Bank reconciliation is the control process that explains differences between bank activity and your internal financial records. When reconciliation is delayed or manual, teams close the month with unanswered questions: Which transactions cleared? Which items are still outstanding? Why does the bank balance differ from the plan?

A clear bank reconciliation workflow reduces errors, improves audit readiness and gives finance teams confidence in cash reporting.

## What is bank reconciliation?

Bank reconciliation compares transactions from a bank statement with the transactions recorded in your accounting or financial operations system. The goal is to confirm that both views agree, or to identify and explain the differences.

Common differences include timing gaps, missing entries, duplicate records, bank fees, currency effects and data entry mistakes. The reconciliation process does not just find mismatches. It creates an evidence trail for the final balance.

### Key terms in statement matching

- **Bank statement:** the bank's record of deposits, withdrawals and fees.
- **Internal record:** the planned or recorded transaction in your system.
- **Outstanding item:** a transaction recorded internally but not yet reflected by the bank, or vice versa.
- **Exception:** a transaction that cannot be matched automatically and needs review.
- **Reconciled balance:** the balance after known timing differences and corrections are explained.

## A step-by-step bank reconciliation process

The right workflow depends on transaction volume, but the control points are consistent.

### 1. Collect the source records

Start with the bank statement for the period and the internal transactions that should relate to that account. Use the same date range and currency context where possible.

### 2. Match transactions

Compare statement lines to internal records. Matches may be based on amount, date, reference, counterparty or internal identifiers. High-volume teams benefit from structured matching rules, but reviewers still need a way to inspect exceptions.

### 3. Investigate exceptions

Not every difference is an error. Common exception types include:

- deposits in transit;
- payments issued but not yet cleared;
- bank charges not recorded internally;
- duplicate imports or duplicate entries;
- incorrect amounts, dates or account allocations.

Each exception should end with an action: match, correct, defer as timing or escalate for review.

### 4. Record adjustments with context

When an internal correction is needed, keep the reason and supporting detail. Reconciliation quality drops when numbers are changed without an audit trail.

### 5. Confirm the reconciled position

Once matched items and explained differences are accounted for, the reconciled position should be clear. Teams should be able to answer what cleared, what is pending and what required adjustment.

## Controls that improve reconciliation quality

Bank reconciliation is both an accounting task and an operational control. A few habits make the process more reliable:

- **Reconcile regularly:** weekly or daily for active accounts; monthly at minimum.
- **Separate duties where possible:** the person preparing activity should not be the only person reviewing exceptions.
- **Preserve source data:** keep statement imports and matching outcomes available for review.
- **Use consistent references:** payment references and transaction metadata improve matching accuracy.
- **Track status:** every line should be matched, pending, corrected or explained.

## Bank reconciliation checklist

Before closing a period, confirm:

- The correct bank statement and date range were used.
- Statement lines were matched or reviewed as exceptions.
- Timing differences are explained, not hidden.
- Corrections have a clear reason and owner.
- The reconciled position can be understood by someone outside the process.

A good reconciliation workflow makes the close faster because questions are answered during the period, not after it.
