A payment approval workflow is a control, but it is also a product decision. Done badly, it creates queues, bypasses and frustration. Done well, it makes the right payments faster because everyone knows what happens next.

The goal is not to approve everything. The goal is to make risky payments visible before money moves.

## Start with the risks

Payment fraud and error usually come from a small set of failure modes:

- A new payee or changed bank details.
- A payment above the approver’s authority.
- One person creating, approving and releasing a payment.
- Duplicate invoices or duplicate payment requests.
- Urgent payments that skip the normal process.
- Approvals given in chat without evidence.
- No record of who changed what.

Design controls around those risks, not around a generic checklist.

## Define roles clearly

Separate permissions into distinct actions:

| Action | Typical owner |
| --- | --- |
| Create a payment request | Requester, department owner |
| Approve a payment | Manager, finance lead |
| Release a payment | Finance operator, admin |
| Change a payee or bank detail | Admin with separate verification |
| Override or expedite | Owner or finance lead, with reason |

The critical rule is segregation of duties: the person who creates the payee or request should not be the only person who approves and releases it.

## Use thresholds, not one approval path

A tiered model keeps small payments fast and large payments controlled:

| Amount | Approval path |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1,000 | Department owner |
| 1,000–10,000 | Finance manager |
| 10,000–50,000 | Finance lead plus department head |
| Above 50,000 | Two approvers, including an owner or admin |

Set thresholds based on your risk tolerance and cash position. Review them quarterly.

## Build the workflow around evidence

Each payment request should carry:

- The business purpose
- The payee and bank account
- The amount and currency
- The related invoice, contract or planned entry
- The project or department owner
- The approval history

If the evidence is missing, the request is not ready. This prevents the common pattern where the approver becomes the person who fixes the paperwork.

## Handle exceptions without bypassing controls

Urgent payments are a reality. The answer is an explicit exception path, not an informal shortcut:

- Who can approve an expedited payment
- What evidence is required after the fact
- Which limits apply
- How the exception is reported

Exceptions should be visible in reporting. A high exception rate is a signal that the standard process is too slow or unclear.

## Protect payee changes

Changing a supplier’s bank details is one of the highest-risk events in accounts payable. Require:

- A separate verification step
- Confirmation through a known channel
- A second approver for the change
- A record of the old and new details

Do not let the same request both change the bank details and release a payment.

## Metrics for payment approval

- **Approval cycle time:** request to approval.
- **Payment cycle time:** approval to release.
- **Exception rate:** payments that bypass the standard path.
- **Rework rate:** requests returned for missing information.
- **Segregation-of-duties violations:** should be zero.
- **Payment success rate:** executed without failure or correction.

## Common design mistakes

- Approving in chat with no record.
- One approver for every payment, regardless of size.
- An “urgent” channel that bypasses all controls.
- Letting requester and approver be the same person for sensitive payments.
- No separate control for new payees.
- Approvals that do not show what is being approved.
- No visibility into pending payments.
