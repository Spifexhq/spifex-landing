Small businesses often postpone financial controls until they hire a finance team. That is backwards. The smaller the team, the more important it is to make responsibilities explicit, because there are fewer people to catch mistakes.

Controls do not need to be bureaucratic. A small set of rules, applied consistently, prevents most avoidable losses.

## The minimum viable control set

For most small businesses, six controls cover the highest risks:

1. **Bank access:** who can view, initiate and approve payments.
2. **Approval limits:** who can approve which amount.
3. **Segregation of duties:** no single person controls a payment end to end.
4. **Payee verification:** new or changed bank details require a separate check.
5. **Reconciliation:** bank movements are matched and reviewed regularly.
6. **Evidence:** approvals, invoices and changes leave a trace.

If those six are working, the business is in a reasonable position. Everything else is refinement.

## 1. Bank access

Review who has access to each bank account and what they can do:

- View balances and statements
- Create payment drafts
- Approve payments
- Manage users and permissions

Remove access promptly when someone leaves or changes role. Use separate accounts for operational and reserve cash where practical.

## 2. Approval limits

Write down approval thresholds and make them visible:

| Amount | Who approves |
| --- | --- |
| Under 1,000 | Department owner |
| 1,000–10,000 | Finance manager |
| 10,000–50,000 | Finance lead plus department head |
| Above 50,000 | Two approvers, including an owner |

Thresholds should be low enough to catch unusual payments but high enough to avoid approving every coffee receipt.

## 3. Segregation of duties

In a small team, perfect separation is not always possible. Use compensating controls when it is not:

- A second reviewer for large or unusual payments.
- A monthly independent review of bank activity.
- A log of who approved what.
- A rule that the person who creates a payee cannot also release the first payment.

Document the exception if one person must hold multiple permissions temporarily.

## 4. Payee verification

Supplier bank-detail fraud is common because it is simple: change the account number and let the next payment go to the wrong place.

Require:

- Verification through a known phone number or portal.
- A second approver for the change.
- A record of the old and new details.
- A hold on the first payment after a change.

## 5. Reconciliation

Reconcile bank accounts regularly, not only at month-end. The longer the gap, the harder it is to reconstruct what happened.

Review:

- Unmatched bank movements
- Unusual or round-number payments
- Payments to new payees
- Duplicate amounts
- Reversals and adjustments

## 6. Evidence

A control that leaves no evidence is difficult to audit and easy to override. Keep:

- Approval history
- Invoice and contract links
- Change logs for payees and bank details
- Reconciliation evidence
- Exception notes and resolution

## A monthly control review

A 30-minute monthly review is enough for many small businesses:

1. Review bank access and recent user changes.
2. Check payments above the top threshold.
3. Review new payees and changed bank details.
4. Look at unmatched reconciliation items.
5. Review exceptions and overrides.
6. Confirm that the approval limits still match the business.

## Warning signs

- The same person can create, approve and release a payment.
- Approvals happen in chat or verbally.
- Bank details can be changed without a second check.
- Reconciliation is always behind.
- Nobody reviews the list of new payees.
- Exceptions are normal rather than exceptional.
