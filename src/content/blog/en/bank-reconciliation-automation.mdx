Bank reconciliation is one of the most important controls in a finance team. It is also one of the first processes to break when volume grows.

Manual reconciliation depends on a person opening a bank statement, scanning a ledger and deciding what matches. That works for a small number of movements. It becomes slow, inconsistent and difficult to audit at scale.

Automation does not remove the need for judgment. It removes the repetitive work so judgment can focus on exceptions.

## What bank reconciliation automation actually does

A good reconciliation workflow has four parts:

1. **Import** bank movements from statements or feeds.
2. **Match** movements to planned entries, payments or invoices using rules.
3. **Explain** unmatched items as exceptions.
4. **Retain evidence** of the match and any changes.

Automation is the rules and workflow around those steps. It should make the matching explainable, not hide it behind a black box.

## Start with matching rules

Useful rules combine several signals:

- Amount and currency
- Direction (inflow or outflow)
- Date window
- Counterparty or payee
- Reference or invoice number
- Bank account
- Movement type

A simple hierarchy works well:

1. **Exact match:** amount, date and reference.
2. **Strong match:** amount and counterparty within a date window.
3. **Suggested match:** amount and probable counterparty.
4. **Manual exception:** no reliable match.

The goal is not to auto-match everything. The goal is to auto-match the obvious items and make the rest easy to resolve.

## Handle exceptions explicitly

Exceptions are where reconciliation creates value. Common categories:

- Partial payments
- Payments combined into one bank transfer
- Bank fees and interest
- FX differences
- Returned or failed payments
- Duplicate movements
- Unplanned spending
- Customer payments without a reference

Each exception needs:

- An owner
- A next action
- A due date
- Supporting evidence

An exception queue without ownership becomes a list of unresolved problems.

## Keep the audit trail

Reconciliation evidence should answer:

- What was matched?
- Who matched it?
- When?
- What rule or evidence was used?
- What changed after the initial match?

If the answer depends on someone’s memory or an email thread, the control is weak.

## Metrics that show whether automation is working

Track:

- **Auto-match rate:** percentage matched without manual intervention.
- **Reconciliation lag:** days from bank movement to match.
- **Exception rate:** unmatched or reworked items as a share of total.
- **Exception resolution time:** how long items stay open.
- **Adjustment rate:** matches changed after initial review.
- **Unplanned movement share:** activity that never had a planned entry.

Improve the process by attacking the largest exception category, not by adding more rules blindly.

## Implementation plan

### Week 1: Baseline

- Export the last three months of bank movements.
- Categorize why items were unmatched or reworked.
- Count the hours spent on reconciliation.

### Week 2: Standardize

- Clean counterparties and references.
- Define the date windows and matching hierarchy.
- Agree exception categories and owners.

### Week 3: Automate the obvious

- Enable exact and strong matches.
- Keep suggested matches under review.
- Do not auto-match new payees or unusual amounts.

### Week 4: Review and tighten

- Measure auto-match and exception rates.
- Remove rules that create false confidence.
- Add controls for sensitive changes.

## What not to automate

Some steps should remain human-reviewed:

- New vendor bank details
- Large or unusual payments
- Manual journal entries that affect cash
- Overrides of suggested matches
- Changes to matching rules

Automation should strengthen controls, not bypass them.
